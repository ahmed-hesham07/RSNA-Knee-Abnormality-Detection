# RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection — Research Report: How Competitors Reach Their Public Scores

Compiled 2026-08-18 from Kaggle's Notebooks (353), Datasets (95), Discussion Topics (55), Comments, and Models (10) tabs for the "RSNA Knee" search, plus the competition's own Overview/Data/Rules pages and live Efficiency leaderboard.

## The competition, in one paragraph

RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection ($77,000 prize, ~1,840 teams, started 2026-07-30, entries close 2026-10-15) asks for a per-study probability on 12 knee MRI findings (ACL, MCL, medial/lateral meniscus, medial/lateral/patellofemoral OA, effusion, synovitis, Baker's cyst, contusion, fracture), scored as macro-averaged ROC-AUC. There are 4,407 training studies and ~1,300 test studies, 569GB of DICOM. The entire competition pivots on one structural fact: **train.csv has a free-text radiology Report for every study, but only 58 of 4,407 studies (1.3%) have real expert per-finding labels, and the Report column does not exist in test.csv.** So report text can only ever be used to manufacture training targets — never as a model input at inference — and the quality of that manufactured label set is the actual bottleneck, not model architecture. Nearly everything interesting in the notebooks, datasets, and discussion threads is downstream of this one fact.

## The honest score picture (don't anchor on notebook votes alone)

Three different "ceilings" are visible in this data, and they matter for interpreting everything below:

- **Public forkable notebooks top out around 0.917–0.920** (Mattia Angeli's 5-family rank-ensemble, and Tony Li's extension of it — both traced to a chain of ~13 credited contributors).
- **The real competition leaderboard (scraped in Ryan Holbrook's live Efficiency-track notebook) tops out at 0.945**, with a dense cluster of teams at 0.93–0.94 (MKhlystun 0.945, Lukas Nissen Molvær 0.943, Brandon Low 0.942, Siontist/JOLEE 0.938, several more at 0.93–0.936). Serious competitors are 1.5–3 AUC points ahead of anything shared publicly — expected, since nobody hands over their real edge mid-competition.
- **A well-tuned single model reaches 0.92–0.94** per direct testimony from top-8 competitor Tucker Arrants (single DINOv2 architecture, 5-fold, 224px, 0.934→0.942) — this number is corroborated independently by the Efficiency leaderboard, where the same person appears at 0.934. Multiple competitors in the "Best single-model score" thread argue the giant 20+model public ensembles are chasing notebook votes/kudos, not the efficient path to a good score.

So: if your goal is to reach the top of the leaderboard, the public notebook ecosystem shows you the *mechanics* (what to ensemble, how to preprocess) but not the actual frontier. The single highest-leverage move visible in this whole dataset is a well-executed single/few-fold DINOv2 model with correct geometry and label handling — not a 35-checkpoint ensemble.

## How the top scores are actually reached — ranked by leverage

### 1. Label quality is the real bottleneck, not the model (highest leverage)
Only 58/4,407 studies have expert labels; everyone else manufactures targets from the Report text via an LLM or a hand-built multilingual lexicon, then trains a pure-imaging model against those manufactured targets. Two independent, rigorously-measured findings converge on this:
- stevenleehans measured that LLM-derived labels score 0.878–0.893 macro AUC against the 58 gold studies, vs. 0.814 for a regex/lexicon approach — a real, replicated gap.
- The community discovered that the LLM's *refusal to answer* ("report doesn't address this," mapped to 0.5) is itself informative and finding-specific: for Baker's cyst, silence strongly means "absent" (3% positive rate when silent vs. 44% when mentioned); for Synovitis, silence is nearly uninformative (34% still positive) because radiologists see it on images but rarely dictate it. Blindly treating "not addressed" as either a hard negative or a coin flip costs real AUC. The fix that worked: use a correlated field (Effusion predicts Synovitis better than Synovitis's own mention rate, because the two co-occur clinically at P=0.63 vs 0.22) to impute *only* the genuinely-uninformative-silence findings, never overriding an explicit statement.
- Blending two *independently produced* label sets (e.g., stevenleehans's own LLM key + lixin73's separately-prompted GPT-5.6-Sol key) beats either alone — and the *weaker* standalone label set adds more lift, because what matters for blending is decorrelation, not standalone quality (0.8780 → 0.8927 macro AUC vs. gold).
- Multilingual handling is non-trivial and cheap to get wrong: reports span ~9 languages/scripts; Turkish negates *after* the term ("efüzyon izlenmedi" = no effusion), so an English-style left-only negation window silently inverts the second-largest language in the corpus. A cheap fix (translate the ~40-60 term vocabulary per finding once, not the whole corpus) gets a lexicon running in minutes on CPU with no GPU cost.
- **Rules note**: the host explicitly ruled that submitting Report text to a commercial LLM API for label extraction is permitted and is not "private data sharing," provided the service is reasonably accessible/low-cost — this is the legal basis for the entire label-engineering ecosystem.

### 2. Imaging geometry and preprocessing beat model/encoder choice (second-highest leverage)
Two independent, rigorous ablations (stevenleehans and FHZ982, using different architectures) reached the *same* conclusion: scaling the encoder from DINOv2-small (22M params) to DINOv2-base (87M params, 3.9x compute) bought +0.001 macro AUC — inside the measured noise floor, i.e., nothing. One competitor even found DINOv3 scored *worse* than DINOv2 on their own pipeline (0.763 vs 0.775). What actually moved their scores:
- **DICOM slice order must be recovered from geometry, never from filename.** The filename is a SOP Instance UID (effectively random); sorting by it matches true anatomical order only ~5% of the time. Correct approach: sort slices by projecting `ImagePositionPatient` onto the normal of `ImageOrientationPatient` (fallback to SliceLocation → InstanceNumber → filename).
- **Crop to a fixed physical field-of-view in millimeters before resizing to pixels, not the other way around.** Field of view varies over 70+ distinct values (median ~160mm) in this dataset, so a fixed-pixel crop does not fix physical scale — a 1-3mm pathology (a tear) can be erased before the first convolution if resolution is too coarse. Values seen in top pipelines: 130mm crop → 336px (~0.39mm/px, Pilkwang Kim's baseline), 150mm crop (Tom Aindow, top-201), 130mm → 336px (FHZ982), 392px/150mm (another top single-model competitor).
- **Laterality (medial/lateral, needed for 5 of the 12 labels) isn't in train.csv and must be derived from imaging geometry**, not assumed — image centre in patient space (not the DICOM corner), with plane-specific normalization rules (coronal/axial need horizontal flip normalization; sagittal needs stack-order normalization).
- **Don't stack many slices as raw channels and average a pretrained conv1's weights** — this destroys the encoder's learned input interface. The convergent best practice: group 3 physically-adjacent slices as one RGB triplet (giving a genuine ~10mm depth neighborhood), run each triplet through the untouched backbone, aggregate features afterward. One ablation found 3 slices beat 9 evenly-spread slices by ~4x their noise floor (though confounded with anchor placement).
- **Checkpoints carry an implicit preprocessing contract** (mean/std normalization constants, native resolution) — swapping to a different pretrained backbone (e.g. RAD-DINO, chest-X-ray pretrained) without reading its actual `preprocessor_config.json` produces a plausible-looking but silently wrong result (one team nearly published a false "medical pretraining doesn't transfer" conclusion caused entirely by this bug).
- Auxiliary legitimate signal: **PatientSex is present in the test DICOM headers** (even though the train.csv column is documented-but-missing) and correlates strongly with labels (males: ACL ~54% vs females ~32%; females: Medial OA ~45% vs males ~12%) — a real, rules-compliant feature. DICOM metadata as a whole was rigorously tested as a potential "shortcut" (Oleksii Zhukov) and found to cap out at ~0.60-0.65 macro AUC even with GroupKFold scanner-holdout validation, far below the 0.90+ image-based scores — so the high public scores are not leaderboard leakage, they're genuine imaging signal.

### 3. Ensembling: rank-averaging, not probability-averaging, and it's mostly collaborative
Because the metric is macro AUC, only the *order* of predictions matters — calibration and thresholds are worthless. Every top notebook independently derives the same conclusion from this and combines models by averaging **percentile ranks**, never raw probabilities (a probability average lets whichever model is most overconfident dominate; rank averages don't). The highest documented public score (0.920, Tony Li) is a nested rank-blend of 35 checkpoints across 5 architecturally distinct families (20 DINOv2-small, 5 DINOv3-small, 2× RadImageNet attention-head variants on a shared ResNet-50 encoder, the second variant re-run on two different slot layouts), with hand-tuned per-label mixing weights (e.g., RadImageNet explicitly excluded from Baker's cyst and Fracture predictions in two independent notebooks — a convergent finding that it doesn't help those two labels). This ensemble is not one person's work — it's a credited chain of at least 13 Kagglers building on each other's checkpoints and label sets over about two weeks.

### 4. What doesn't work / was tested and rejected
- Bigger encoder (DINOv2-base, DINOv3) — no measurable gain over DINOv2-small, sometimes worse (see #2).
- DICOM metadata as a scoring shortcut — caps at ~0.60-0.65, not competitive.
- "Soft"/graded report-derived labels vs. hard-binarized ones — a very rigorous pre-registered A/B (FHZ982) found soft labels win on the 58 gold studies in 3/3 seeds but the confidence interval crosses zero (not statistically conclusive at 58 studies); the proposed causal mechanism for why soft should help failed its own pre-specified check. Genuinely unresolved, reported honestly rather than oversold.
- Universal cross-label imputation (ridge-predicting each of the 12 labels from the other 11 to fill "not addressed" cells) — worse overall than targeted, finding-specific imputation, because for some findings silence is a real informative negative and for others it's noise.

## Community dynamics worth knowing before you trust a vote count
Vote counts on these notebooks are not a clean signal of quality or originality. A well-documented forum thread (Mattia Angeli, "Does Kaggle Do Anything About Byte-for-Byte Public Notebook Theft?") shows some accounts republish others' notebooks byte-for-byte with credits stripped, and Kaggle's "fork = automatic vote" mechanic lets this farm medals/tier progression with zero original work — confirmed by an independent AI-assisted similarity scan the same author ran across the public notebook pool. This matches the raw pattern I found in the listing: of 353 "RSNA Knee" notebooks, votes drop from 363 to single digits by around the 40th-ranked notebook, and the long tail (150-250 notebooks) is overwhelmingly near-duplicate DINOv2/DINOv3 forks and templated EDA notebooks with zero comments — not independent solutions.

## Tab-by-tab pass: what Input/Output/Logs/Comments add beyond the Notebook tab

Following up on the first pass, I went back through the top-ranked notebooks tab-by-tab (Input, Output, Logs, Comments — not just the rendered Notebook body), plus verified the official competition Prizes/Rules page directly. Three findings changed or sharpened the picture above; the rest confirmed it with harder evidence.

**A meaningful fraction of the highest-vote "ensemble" notebooks are not currently in the state that produced their displayed score.** Checking the Logs tab (which shows Accelerator and actual runtime) on Sakhawat Hossen's "RSNA Knee Master Ensemble" (86 votes, 0.911) and Tony Li's "DINO-RadImageNet Rank Ensemble" (0.920, the highest public score in this whole search) found both committed versions ran in ~6-12 seconds with **Accelerator: None** and **0 bytes of output** — i.e. the code currently visible on the page never actually executed the GPU pipeline it describes; only markdown/definition cells re-rendered on save. Their displayed scores come from an earlier or different commit. By contrast, Pilkwang's baseline, prvsiyan's, Will/wguesdon's, Mattia Angeli's, aadigupta's, and Roman Rozen's notebooks all show fully-executed multi-thousand-second GPU logs matching their claims. **Practical takeaway: before trusting a forked notebook's code as "the recipe that got 0.92," check its Logs tab for Accelerator ≠ None and non-trivial runtime — several of the most-forked notebooks in this ecosystem fail that check.** This is a sharper, more damning version of the earlier "prvsiyan's BiomedCLIP/ViTDet branches encoded 0 slices" finding — same underlying problem (a notebook's narrated sophistication outrunning what it actually ran), now confirmed as systemic rather than a one-off.

**The official competition Prizes page (verified live) contradicts a stale community complaint.** Comments on Ryan Holbrook's Efficiency-LB notebook (from ~9 days before this check) claimed the Efficiency Prize rules page was broken/missing. It isn't — I loaded it directly. Full mechanics: Efficiency Score = AUC/(Benchmark − maxAUC) + RuntimeSeconds/32400 (lower is better; 32400s = the 9-hour runtime cap), eligible only for a team's selected Leaderboard-Prize submissions that beat the sample_submission.csv benchmark on the private LB. Prizes: main leaderboard $9,000 down to $5,000 across 10 places, efficiency track $7,000/$6,000/$5,000 (total $77,000 confirmed). Also notable: **Ryan Holbrook is listed as an official co-organizer in the competition's own citation block**, not a community member — which is why his leaderboard-scraping notebook runs on a schedule and is treated as authoritative.

**A genuinely novel technical idea not covered in the first pass:** Will/wguesdon's notebook (84 votes, only 0.815 public score but the most rigorous methodology read in this whole project) makes and empirically verifies a Nyquist-sampling argument against the community's default 336px/14px-patch DINOv2 setup — a d-mm lesion needs pixel pitch ≤d/2mm to survive the patch tokenizer, so a 1-3mm meniscal tear is physically unrepresentable at low input resolution regardless of model quality; verified with a synthetic lesion-painting probe on the frozen backbone (1mm lesions gain 3.4x embedding sensitivity going 126px→336px; 10mm lesions gain nothing, as predicted). Separately, aadigupta's "Let Me Cook" (0.899) contributes a clean, well-reasoned per-finding pooling rule — max/top-2-window pooling for focal pathology (fractures, tears, Baker's cysts) vs. mean-of-all-windows for diffuse findings (OA, effusion) — reasoned from the same signal-dilution logic. Neither of these ideas shows up in the highest-vote notebooks, which favor scale (more checkpoints) over these lower-cost, higher-insight fixes.

**Confirmations with harder evidence:** the same 20 DINOv2 checkpoints (identical weight-fingerprint hashes, e.g. `e5427d6c21`, `d47313baef`) load unmodified across at least 3 independent notebooks in this pass, byte-confirming Pilkwang's weights dataset really is shared verbatim (not independently retrained) across its 90 dependent notebooks. Roman Rozen's 100-vote notebook — labeled "EDA, baseline" on Kaggle — is internally titled "Twelve findings from one knee MRI — v4," i.e. it's a full modeling pipeline mislabeled as EDA; its fully-executed 101-minute training log gives a concrete honest-OOF number (0.7675 macro AUC on the 58 gold studies) against its own 0.894 public score, a clean single-notebook illustration of the "three ceilings" gap already central to this report. Its own changelog is worth flagging as a good practices list: no vertical-flip augmentation (flipping swaps femoral condyle/tibial plateau, corrupting the 3 OA labels), a pairwise ranking loss term added alongside BCE to directly optimize the AUC-shaped metric (the only notebook seen doing this at training time rather than only at ensemble time), and laterality-fallback enablement gated on a measured tag-vs-geometry agreement rate (91.4% over 2,050 studies) rather than assumed.

## My read on this, for what it's worth

If you're planning to compete rather than just spectate: the public notebook ecosystem is a good place to steal the *plumbing* (DICOM slice-order recovery, physical-mm cropping, rank-based ensembling, the report-label bottleneck) but a bad place to steal the *edge* — the actual leaderboard is running 2-3 AUC points ahead of anything public, and the highest-vote notebooks are disproportionately forks and vote-farming rather than novel work. The highest-leverage use of your time, based on everything above, is (1) build your own report-label pipeline rather than trusting a single public CSV blindly — decorrelated label sets blend better than any one "best" set — and (2) get the imaging geometry exactly right (true slice order, physical-mm cropping, correct laterality) before touching model size, since two independent rigorous ablations agree encoder scale is close to irrelevant here. A single well-tuned DINOv2-small model with 5-fold CV, done carefully, is already competitive with the public 35-model ensembles, per the top competitors' own testimony.
